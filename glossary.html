FDE Glossary

House definitions. Where a term is contested, the contest is noted. Lessons use these spellings and these meanings; if a lesson needs a different one, the glossary is wrong and gets fixed.

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) — An engineer who works inside a customer’s environment and is accountable for whether the deployed system works and gets used, not for delivering a spec. The term is Shyam Sankar’s, coined at Palantir c. 2006; “forward deployed” is borrowed from military usage, meaning stationed at the point of action rather than at a rear base. Palantir’s own title is FDSE (Forward Deployed Software Engineer). Anthropic files the role under Applied AI.

Echo / Delta — Palantir’s split of the forward-deployed function. Echo finds the right problem and holds the customer relationship (embedded analysts, domain people). Delta turns the answer into working software on site (prototyping engineers). At smaller companies one person is both, which is the version you are training for.

PD (Product Development) — The non-deployed engineering org that generalises what FDEs build for one customer into product that serves many. The FDE/PD handoff is the mechanism that keeps the model from being consulting. Qureshi: “Your job was to solve the problem, and not worry about overfitting; PD’s job was to take whatever you’d built and generalize it.”

Capability–application gap — The distance between what a frontier model can do and what a specific enterprise workflow needs. The reason AI labs hire FDEs: the gap is closed per-customer, by someone who can both read the workflow and write the code.

Cut sheet — (House term, this course.) The five-line scope of a first deployment: one user, one workflow, one baseline, one thin slice, one kill criterion. Each line is one sentence and every line must be fillable from evidence, not from the customer’s aspiration.

Thin slice (also walking skeleton) — The smallest end-to-end artifact that moves the baseline number for the named user. End-to-end is the load-bearing part: a slice that stops at a demo is a prototype, not a slice.

Baseline — What the workflow costs today, measured. Time per instance, instances per week, error or rework rate, cost. An executive’s estimate is not a baseline; it is a hypothesis about the baseline, and should be labelled as one until someone counts.

Kill criterion — The number that, unmet by a stated date, ends the pilot. Agreed with the customer in writing before work starts. Its purpose is not pessimism — it is the thing that makes the success number mean something.

Pilot — A time-boxed first deployment with real users and real data. Distinguished from a POC (proof of concept), which uses sample data and proves only that the technology runs. Most of the industry uses the words interchangeably; this course does not.

Pilot purgatory — The state of a deployment that works, is liked, and never enters production because no one owns the budget, the security review, or the on-call. The MIT NANDA study’s 95% live here. Avoiding it is a scoping decision made before the pilot starts, not a sales push after.

Time to first value (TTFV) — Days from engagement start to the first moment the named user gets a result they would miss if you took it away. The FDE metric that correlates with renewal.

Design partner — An early customer who gets access and influence in exchange for deep access to their workflow and tolerance for roughness. The pre-product version of a deployment.

Land and expand — Win one workflow, prove the number, then use that proof to take the next workflow in the same organisation. The commercial logic the cut sheet serves: a narrow first deployment is not a small ambition, it is the cheapest route to a large one.

Consulting death spiral — When per-customer work never generalises, so revenue scales only with headcount and the product never improves. The structural risk of the FDE model; the defence is the FDE→PD feedback path and a deliberate count of what became product.

Product leverage — How much of what you built for one customer serves the next one. The second half of an FDE’s performance review, and the half candidates forget to mention.

White-glove deployment — Hands-on, high-touch delivery into an enterprise environment: their VPC or hardware, their identity provider, their review boards. Named explicitly in Anthropic’s FDE job description.

Ontology — Palantir’s term for the mapping between a customer’s real-world entities and the data model the software operates on. Generalise the idea, not the product: every deployment has an ontology problem, and discovering it is usually the hardest week.

Heretic — Sankar’s hiring heuristic: the person inside a domain who already believes the standard way is wrong. Makes a good Echo because they will tell you where the bodies are.

“Doing things that don’t scale, at scale” — McGrew’s description of the FDE model: the per-customer work is deliberately unscalable, and the company is built so that it still compounds.
