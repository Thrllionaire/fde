The Cut

You can already build the thing. This workspace is about the half-hour before you are allowed to: a room, an executive with a vague ask, and the decision about what the first deployment actually is. Get the cut wrong and the build is wasted no matter how good it is. Today: five lines that turn an ask into a deployment, and the two of them that are hard.

The gap you are hired into

Frontier labs hire FDEs for a specific reason, and it is worth being able to say it in one sentence at a recruiter screen. Anjor Kanekar, seven years an FDE at Palantir, puts it as well as anyone: “There is a gap between foundational model capabilities and the application of those in enterprise use cases where they can add value.” The model is general. The value is specific. Somebody has to stand in the specific place and close the distance, and that person has to be able to both read the workflow and write the code — which is why it is an engineer and not an account manager.

Bob McGrew, who helped invent the role at Palantir before running research at OpenAI, describes the model as “doing things that don't scale, at scale.” That is a statement about economics, and it has a consequence you feel on day one: the per-customer work is expensive, so the first deployment has to be small and it has to land. Those two constraints together are what the cut is for.

Why the cut is the job and not the preamble

The most-quoted number in enterprise AI is that 95% of generative-AI pilots produce no measurable P&L impact. It comes from MIT Media Lab's Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025 — 300-plus publicly disclosed initiatives, 52 structured interviews, 153 survey responses, gathered January to June 2025. Use the number carefully; it is not a random sample of enterprises and the figure gets flung around by people who have not read the method.

But the finding underneath it is the one that matters to you, and it is not the headline. The authors attribute the failures to brittle workflows, weak contextual learning, and poor fit with daily operations — not to model capability. The pilots did not fail because the model was not smart enough. They failed because somebody picked the wrong workflow, or picked a real workflow and never found out what it cost before they changed it, or built something nobody could tell was working.

Those are all scoping failures. They happen before the first line of code and they are unrecoverable afterwards. This is the good news about the role: the highest-leverage thing you do all engagement is a decision you make in week one with no code at all.

The cut sheet: five lines

Here is the artifact. Five lines, one sentence each, on one page. You write it in week one, you show it to the customer, and you do not start building until every line is filled from evidence rather than from somebody's hopes.

LineThe question that produces itFailure mode
One user Who, by name, does this today? Could I email them this afternoon? A department. “The ops team” has no workflow and cannot be shadowed.
One workflow What do they do, how many times a week, starting with what and ending with what? A category. “Claims” is a dozen workflows wearing one coat.
One baseline What does it cost today, and who counted? An estimate repeated until it sounds measured. See below.
One thin slice What is the smallest thing that runs end to end and moves that number? A demo. A slice that stops short of the user's real input is a prototype.
One kill criterion What number, unmet by what date, ends this? Absent, because nobody wanted to say it out loud.

Three of these are easy once you know to ask. Two are not, and the two hard ones are where candidates lose the decomposition round.

The baseline is almost never a number yet

When you ask what a workflow costs today, you will get a number. It will be confident, round, and sourced from nobody. “About twenty minutes.” “Roughly two million a year.” These are not baselines, they are hypotheses about the baseline, and the difference decides whether your pilot can be judged at the end.

The move is not to argue. It is to write the line with its provenance attached:

# not this
Baseline: 20 minutes per quote.

# this
Baseline: UNMEASURED. VP Ops estimates ~20 min/quote; no instrumentation.
          Week 1: time 6 real quotes with Priya before the slice is agreed.

Writing UNMEASURED on a page you hand an executive feels rude the first time. It is the single most credible thing on the sheet. It says you intend the success number to mean something, and it buys you the one week you need to make that true.

The trap

The baseline is the line that quietly decides the fate of the pilot, which is why it is the line everyone fills in fastest. An unmeasured baseline cannot be beaten — it can only be argued with, six months later, by a CFO who was not in the room. If you take one habit from this lesson, take this: never write a number on the baseline line that you cannot say who counted.

The kill criterion is a gift, not a threat

Asking a customer to agree in writing what would make this a failure sounds like the fastest way to lose the room. It is the opposite, for a reason worth internalising: a success number with no failure number attached is unfalsifiable, and everybody in the room knows it. Proposing the kill criterion is the move that marks you as someone who intends to be measured.

It also protects you. The thing that kills FDE engagements is not failure, it is pilot purgatory — the deployment that works, is liked, and never goes to production because no one ever defined what crossing the line looked like. A date with a number on it is the exit from purgatory in both directions.

Where this came from

The cut has to be small enough to re-cut. When Shyam Sankar invented the model in 2006 he flew to Virginia every Sunday, demoed to CIA and DIA analysts Monday through Thursday, flew back Friday, and the team coded over the weekend — a one-week loop. A scope you cannot revisit in a week is not a first deployment; it is a bet. Keep the slice inside the loop you can actually run.

Drill: what is your next move?

Six moments from real-shaped kickoffs. In each, you have one move left before the meeting ends. The option set is the same every time, so the wording gives nothing away — only the situation does.

Drill: write the sheet

Recognition is not production. Write the five lines blind — the criteria appear only after you commit, and the model answer after that.

Build: cut a real one

The drills are recognition and a warm-up. The deliverable is in practice/01-the-cut/. TRANSCRIPT.md is a kickoff meeting at a mid-market industrial distributor, written the way these actually go: a loud executive, a quiet person who does the work, two numbers, and a lot of noise. NOTES.md is your worksheet.

  1. Read the transcript once, then close it and write down what you think the project is. One sentence. Date it. You will want this later.
  2. Write the cut sheet. Five lines, in the file, in full sentences. Not a summary of what the lines would say — the lines.
  3. Name what you cannot fill. At least one line cannot be honestly completed from the transcript alone. Say which, say why, and write the exact question you would ask to fill it — the words you would say, not the topic you would raise.
  4. Defend it. Three hostile questions are in NOTES.md. Answer them in writing before you bring the sheet to me.
  5. Compare step 4's answers against step 1's sentence. If they disagree, that gap is the lesson, and it is the one to bring to the debrief.
House rule, carried over

When a step asks for a sentence, the deliverable is the sentence. In the agent workspace this took four lessons to settle: a verdict about what you would write is not what you would write, and only one of the two can be graded. Everything in this course is prose, so the rule bites harder here.
