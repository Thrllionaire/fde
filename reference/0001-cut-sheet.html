The Cut Sheet

One page. Print it. The five lines of a first deployment, the questions that produce each one, what disqualifies an answer, and the shape of the interview round that tests all of it.

The five lines

One user:      <a person you could email this afternoon>
One workflow:  <start → end, N times per week>
One baseline:  <measured number + who counted + how>  |  UNMEASURED + the plan to fix that
One slice:     <smallest thing that runs end to end on their real input>
One kill:      <number + date that ends the pilot>

Question bank

LineAskDisqualifies the answer
User Who does this today? What is their name? Can I sit with them this week?
Who notices first when it goes wrong?		 A team, a role plural, a persona, a job title with no body attached.
Workflow Walk me through the last one you did. What triggered it? What did you have open? What made it end? How many this week? A noun ("claims", "onboarding", "reporting"). No trigger. No frequency.
Baseline How long does one take? Who timed it? Over what period? How often is it redone or corrected? What does the system log say? A round number nobody counted. An average over several different workflows. A figure sourced to a board deck.
Slice What is the smallest version that this person would use on Monday, on their real inputs? What can stay manual in v1? Anything ending at a demo, a dashboard, or a model evaluation. Anything whose first user is not the named user.
Kill If this number has not moved by this date, do we stop? Who decides? What happens to the deployment if we do? A target with no floor. A date with no number. Nobody empowered to call it.

Red flags in the room

  • The named workflow and the done workflow differ. The executive's ask is a solution they have already imagined. The user's pain is somewhere else in the building. Both are real; only one has a baseline.
  • The number arrived pre-rounded. Twenty minutes, two million, thirty percent. Ask who counted. Write the answer down next to the number, always.
  • Nobody in the room does the work. If the person who performs the workflow is not present and cannot be scheduled, you do not have a deployment, you have a brief.
  • A data-class blocker with a calendar on it. Review boards, data residency, an unapproved model class. These do not get narrowed around; they get solved first or the scope moves to data you are allowed to touch.
  • Enthusiastic scope expansion. "And once that works we could also…" is a good sign about the relationship and a bad sign about the sheet. Write it in a parking lot section; it is Lesson 07's material, not this pilot's.

What an unmeasured baseline is allowed to look like

Baseline: UNMEASURED.
          <who> estimates <figure>; no instrumentation exists.
          Week 1: <who> will <count what> over <period> before the slice is agreed.

Three parts, all required: the admission, the provenance of the guess, and the dated plan that replaces it. Any one of the three missing and it reads as either sloppiness or hedging.

The decomposition round

The open-ended case study is consistently reported as the highest-failure stage of the FDE loop (Exponent, 2026 — secondary, but corroborated across write-ups). The prompts look like: "A regional bank wants to unify fraud detection across three acquired systems with inconsistent labels. Scope the first 90 days." You have 45–60 minutes and no further information unless you ask for it.

The cut sheet is the answer, delivered out loud, in this order:

  1. Clarify before solving. Three to five questions, each one aimed at a specific line of the sheet. Say which line you are filling.
  2. Name your assumptions as assumptions. Out loud, labelled. Interviewers are scoring whether you know which of your inputs are evidence and which are guesses.
  3. Map the stakeholders. Who does the work, who pays, who can block. The blocker is the one candidates forget and the one that kills real deployments.
  4. Sequence by risk, not by dependency. Do the thing first that would most cheaply prove the whole idea wrong.
  5. Propose the slice, then the kill criterion. Volunteering the failure condition unprompted is the strongest single move available in this round.
If you are given no information at all

Say what you would need, pick the most plausible version, and label it: "I'll assume the three systems have no shared customer key, because that is the usual case and it is also the worst case — tell me if that's wrong." Then proceed. Stalling for information reads as inability to act under ambiguity, which is the trait the round exists to measure.

Lines to have ready verbatim

  • Why FDE and not SWE? — "Because I want to be accountable for whether the thing works in production, not for whether I shipped the ticket."
  • Why do AI labs need FDEs? — the capability–application gap: the model is general, the value is specific, and closing that distance needs someone who can read the workflow and write the code.
  • Why do pilots fail? — MIT NANDA attributes it to brittle workflows and poor fit with daily operations, not to model capability. Application-layer problem; that is the job.
